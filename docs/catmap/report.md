# Where longevity research puts its work — and where the money does not follow

**The question.** Which parts of this field are structurally thin, and where does the money sit
against the literature? It is answered here for someone deciding where to look, not for someone
browsing a map: every claim names the shelf it rests on, and the shelves are nodes on the map
beside this document.

## The field is six functions, and one of them is most of the literature

The map cuts longevity research by **what a piece of work does for the goal**, not by discipline
or mechanism. That gives six functions: `mechanism` (read the damage), `measurement` (locate a
body in time), `interventions` (change the trajectory), `translation` (turn a finding into
something a clinic can do), `accelerants` (make the research itself faster) and `escape`
(preservation and substrate).

Summed across their 78 classes, the six carry **264,900 "thousands of works"** of attention —
the count of works, 2018–2026, whose title carries a shelf's own vocabulary and whose abstract
names aging. **Interventions alone carries 148,400 of that, 56%** (`interventions`). Mechanisms
carries 45,200 (17%), measurement 28,900 (11%), translation 19,000 (7%), preservation 13,100
(5%), accelerants 10,300 (4%).

The concentration is sharper than those numbers suggest, because three shelves inside
interventions are almost the whole of it: **diet 52,800, sleep 41,200, exercise 32,500** —
together **126,500, or 48% of everything on this map** (`diet`, `sleep`, `exercise`). Whatever
the molecular literature says about geroprotectors, the field's centre of gravity is lifestyle,
by an order of magnitude: `rapamycin-mtor` carries 900 and `nad-plus` 1,500, together 0.9% of
the map.

## Mechanisms: the most cited, and the least growing

The mechanism shelves hold the papers other papers build on — a median of **210 citations** per
work, against 168 for interventions and 52 for preservation. They are also the oldest in
attention: of the 325 works filed under `mechanism`, **22 (7%) are from 2024 or later**, against
20% for interventions and 48% for accelerants. A field reading its own foundations is not the
same as a field moving; measured this way, mechanisms is where the map is most settled.

It is also where the map records the most retractions: **17 of the 52 retracted works** on the
whole map sit under mechanisms, against 20 under interventions, 9 under translation, 6 under
measurement. The dark-grey mark is the index's, not an opinion: a retracted paper or a title that
states a null result.

## Money is not where the literature is

Declared funder weight on this map is **333.5 units of US$10M** — award-level amounts for the
works that are actually here, not a body's total budget. Apportioning each funder's weight across
the works it paid for gives: **measurement 113.4 (34%), interventions 103.8 (31%), mechanisms
68.2 (20%), preservation 31.3 (9%), accelerants 12.1 (4%), translation 4.6 (1.4%)**.

Two things follow. First, **the NIH family carries half of the award weight attached to these
works (49.9%)**, and the National Institute on Aging alone 27% — that is a share of this corpus,
not of either agency's budget. Second, and more
useful for a reader: **translation is the shelf with the most companies and the least public
money**. Seven of the map's seventeen companies target `translation` — more than any other
function — while the function takes 1.4% of the apportioned funding and has a median citation
count of 87, the second lowest on the map. Private capital is doing the work that the funding
record does not show.

The mirror image is `escape`: 264 works, 19 classes, 26% of them since 2024 — and **not one
company**. Preservation is growing on public and philanthropic money alone.

The private side is thin in absolute terms and concentrated in a few names. Seventeen companies
appear on the map; six have published work here — Calico 18, Unity Biotechnology 12, Insilico
Medicine 12, Altos Labs 10, Elysium Health 7, ChromaDex 7 (`cI4405261977`, `cI4210092599`,
`cI4401200273`, `cI4387153853`, `cI4405267101`, `cI4210151472`). The other eleven are declared
targets without a paper on this map, which is not the same as inactivity: it is the limit of what
a literature-built map can see, and the reason every company is drawn at a floor size. What can be
said is narrower and sturdier than a market estimate: **the companies that do publish sit on
senescence, clocks and trial machinery, and none sits on preservation.**

## The work is concentrated in a handful of institutions

Eight institutions carry 458 of the 1,563 works on this map — **29%** — and each of them works on
two shelves rather than one. Harvard (103) is on clocks and gene therapy (`methylation-clocks`,
`gene-therapy`); Mayo (65) on senescence and implantable interfaces (`senescence`,
`ecog-endovascular`); Stanford (60) on those interfaces and proteomic clocks (`ecog-endovascular`,
`proteomic-clocks`); Oxford (50) on platform trials and cohorts (`platform-trials`, `biobanks`);
Inserm (48) on aging as an indication and pharmacovigilance (`aging-indication`,
`pharmacovigilance`); Washington University (45) on gene therapy and interfaces; UCSF (44) on
biomarker qualification and clocks (`biomarker-qualification`); Washington (43) on the LEV account
and interfaces (`lev-account`).

Two readings. First, the clock shelves and the implantable-interface shelf are the map's
institutional magnets: four of the top eight list `ecog-endovascular`, three list a clock. Second,
the remaining 22 institutions carry the rest between them, one shelf at a time — the same
concentration the attention figures show, repeated among the organisations.

## The fresh edge is tools, not biology

Where the map is newest is not where it is largest. `accelerants` — biobanks, cohorts, structure
prediction, self-driving laboratories — has **48% of its works from 2024 or later**, the highest
of the six functions, on 4% of the attention. `translation` is next at 23%, `escape` at 26%,
interventions at 20%, measurement at 21%, mechanisms at 7%.

Thinness has a shape too. **Thirty-one of the 78 classes carry fewer than 500 works**, and ten of
them carry none at all in this corpus: `epigenetic-editing`, `virtual-cell`,
`self-driving-labs`, `xenotransplant`, `bioartificial-organs`, `cryoprotectants`, `nanowarming`,
`cryonics-storage`, `emulation-modelling`. Ten classes carry 5,000 or more, and they are the
lifestyle shelves plus `frailty`, `microbiome`, `longevity-genetics`, `stem-cell-therapy`,
`mouse-translation` and `biobanks`. The distribution is not a bell: it is three shelves and a
long tail of bets.

## What a reader should take from this

- **If you are looking for where the field's effort is**, it is lifestyle intervention, and the
  molecular geroprotector literature is a small fraction of it (`diet`, `sleep`, `exercise`).
- **If you are looking for where the evidence is thickest per paper**, it is mechanisms — and it
  is the least recently renewed (`mechanism`).
- **If you are looking for where private capital is ahead of the public record**, it is
  translation: seven companies, 1.4% of the money (`translation`).
- **If you are looking for an unclaimed edge**, preservation has growth and no companies at all
  (`escape`), and thirty-one classes sit under 500 works, several of them at zero.

## What would change these findings, and what that costs

Four things in this map are thinner than the claims above, and each has a known fix rather than a
caveat. Naming the fix is the point: it says what to doubt and what to spend to stop doubting.

**1. The money is apportioned, not read.** Funder weight is an award-level total distributed
across the works that funder paid for, so a funder with one paper here is spread thin by
arithmetic. Fix: pull the actual award amounts for these 198 works from NIH RePORTER (free API,
one query per funder), and sum by shelf. Cost: about a day, no licences. What it changes: the
1.4% figure for translation could move by a factor of two; the ranking of measurement and
mechanism above translation would have to be wrong by an implausible margin to flip.

**2. Eleven of seventeen companies have no paper here.** A company without a publication is
invisible to a literature-built map, so "no company in preservation" means "no company in
preservation *that publishes*". Fix: read the pipeline pages and 10-K filings of the six that do
publish, and the press releases of the eleven that do not — free, about two hours — or drop the
company sentence and keep the funding one. What it changes: nothing about the shelves; it makes
the commercial claim either sourced or absent.

**3. Attention counts title vocabulary, and broad words win.** "Diet" appears in the title of a
paper about almost anything nutritional; "senolytics" does not. Fix: re-count every shelf against
OpenAlex topic assignments rather than title terms — free, 110 queries, two hours — and report the
two counts side by side. What it changes: the exact split between lifestyle and molecular shelves
would move; the direction (lifestyle dominating by an order of magnitude) is too large a gap for
this artefact to explain away.

**4. Each shelf was searched once, most-cited first.** Thirty-one classes under 500 works may be
the field's thin edge or the query's. Nothing here needs a redesign to re-run — the class list is
the query set, one request per class — so a second pass is a loop over it, not a rebuild. Fix: a second query per class with five synonyms, and a
comparison of what the first query missed — free, about 400 queries at the same rate as the
original run. What it changes: any class that doubles is a query artefact, not a finding; the ones
that stay under 500 after a second pass are the real tail.

Until those four are done, the findings that carry the report are the ones that do not depend on
them: **where the mass of the literature sits**, **how concentrated it is in a few institutions and
one agency**, and **which shelves no one is publishing on at all**. Those survive all four fixes;
the money split and the company claim are the two that do not, yet.
